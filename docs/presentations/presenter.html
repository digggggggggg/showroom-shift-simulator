LONDON PAINT SHOWROOM

One associate.
One till. One shaker.

A two-round shift simulator, and the case for routing large mixes to a 2-hour London hub

Play at showroom-shift-simulator.vercel.app

Presenter deck. Use these slides around the live game, not instead of it. The flow is: set the scene, play Round 1, debrief the bottleneck, play Round 2 with the hub, then compare the two rounds and open the discussion. Allow about 15 to 20 minutes including discussion; each round of play lasts 30 seconds.

Before the session: open the game full screen on the shared display and turn sound on; the effects are soft and boardroom safe, and the walkout sound is a useful cue for the room. Pick a volunteer who has not seen the game. Let the same person play both rounds so the comparison is fair. Customers arrive at random, so every run differs; that is fine, the pattern repeats.

Each arriving customer is drawn at random with these odds. Large trade mixes are the big tickets, between £420 and £700. Small mixes are £120 to £300. Consultations and sample pots are small tickets, but they are quick, they need only the till, and in the story they are the start of a relationship. Make sure the room knows the first customer is always a 10-tin trade order: that is the dilemma the game is built around.

THE RULES OF THE SHOP

1 s

of shaking per tin, in store, plus the walk to and from the shaker

11 s

of patience per customer while someone is at the till

1.8x

faster patience drain when the counter is unmanned

2.25 s

between new arrivals, up to five in the queue

These four numbers drive everything. Mixing a 10-tin order keeps the associate away for ten seconds of shaking plus the walk. While the counter is empty, every waiting customer loses patience 1.8 times as fast, so 11 seconds of patience shrinks to about 6. Meanwhile a new customer arrives roughly every two and a quarter seconds. You do not need to do the arithmetic for the room; the point is that a long mix and a short fuse cannot coexist.

ROUND 1 - IN-STORE MIXING

Every tin is mixed on site

Say before pressing start

  • "This is how the showroom works today."
  • "Your first customer wants 10 tins. Do you mix it?"
  • "Watch the patience bars while the associate is at the back."

The briefing card in the game says the same: mixing is on site at 1 second per tin, and an unmanned counter strands waiting clients.

Ask the volunteer, out loud, whether they will take the 10-tin order. Almost everyone does: it is £700 and it is right in front of them. That choice is the point. Do not coach the player during the round; let the room see what happens naturally. Press START ROUND 1 when ready.

When the first walkout happens there is a falling coins sound and a red minus figure; let the room react. Notice who walks out: often the quick advice and sample-pot customers, who needed only a moment at the till.

ROUND 1 - DEBRIEF

The bottleneck is the associate's time, not demand

Read from the report

  • Revenue captured
  • Lost to walkouts, and how many
  • Seconds the counter was unmanned, out of 30

Ask the room

  • Who walked out, and what did they want?
  • Was saying yes to 10 tins wrong?
  • Where does this happen in our stores?

The Bottleneck Report shows four lines: revenue captured, lost to walkouts, the number of walkouts, and how long the counter was unmanned out of 30 seconds. Read them out. Then make the central point: there were customers and there was money on the floor, but one person cannot be at the shaker and the till at once. Saying yes to the trade order was not a mistake; the model forces an impossible choice. Draw out examples from the room before moving on.

ROUND 2 - THE 2-HOUR LONDON HUB

One rule changes: big mixes go to the hub

More than 5 tins

Dispatched to the hub in one press. The associate stays at the till.

5 tins or fewer

Still mixed on site, 1 second per tin, exactly as before.

Same player, same customers, same opening 10-tin order.

Introduce Round 2 as the only change to the shop. Orders of more than five tins can now be dispatched to the 2-hour London Hub. The mix button turns blue and reads DISPATCH N TINS TO HUB. Small orders still need the shaker, so the associate will still step away sometimes; that keeps the comparison honest. Everything else, the arrival rate, the patience and the values, stays the same.

KEY TAKEAWAY

Route large mixes off site, and the associate stays where the customers are.

The counter stays staffed, quick consultations get served, and walkouts stop.

This is the one sentence to leave the room with. The hub does not make the associate faster; it takes the slowest job out of the store. That frees the scarcest resource, time at the counter, for the customers who need a person: colour advice, sample pots, the small orders that start a project. If you only have time for one slide after the game, use this one.

HONEST FRAMING

What the model leaves out

Hub costs

Delivery, van capacity and hub staffing are not in the game.

The 2-hour promise

The game assumes trade customers accept delivery instead of waiting.

One associate

Real stores may have a second person or a dedicated mixing bay.

Illustrative numbers

Prices, patience and arrival rates are settings, not measurements.

Say this before someone in the room does. The simulator is built to make one trade-off visible, not to forecast revenue. It leaves out what the hub costs to run, whether trade customers really accept a 2-hour delivery instead of taking tins away, and stores with more than one person on shift. The figures are tuning settings chosen to illustrate the point. The next step after the game is real data: mix times, queue lengths and walkout rates from our own showrooms.

DISCUSSION

Questions to take back to the business

  1. Where is the 5-tin threshold right for us?
  2. What would we measure first: mix time, queue length or walkouts?
  3. Which showrooms feel this bottleneck most?
  4. What would a trade customer need to trust a 2-hour delivery?

Close by turning the game into decisions. The threshold of more than five tins is a setting in the simulator, not a recommendation; ask where it should sit in practice. Ask what data would confirm or kill the idea. Ask which stores to pilot in. And ask what the trade customer would need to hear to accept delivery instead of waiting.
